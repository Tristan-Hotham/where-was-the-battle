Where was the battle?

Some of the most famous battles in British history have no known site. I gathered {{n_candidates|int}} places that have been proposed for {{n_battles|int}} of them, measured the ground at each, and asked a simple question: which ones fit what the earliest texts actually say?

Ask where Mount Badon was fought and you will be offered Bath, a hillfort near Swindon, a hillfort in Dorset, a hill in West Lothian and a village in Lincolnshire. Ask about Brunanburh, the great battle of 937, and you can choose between the Wirral, Dumfriesshire, the Humber and Cambridgeshire. For many battles before 1066 the chroniclers named a place that no longer exists, or a hill, a river or a fort with no further address.

So I tried something modest. For each battle I wrote down only what the early texts actually say about the place: a hill, a siege, ships, a river, a lake, a named town nearby. I turned each clue into a check I can compute from open data, measured the ground at every proposed site, and scored them. There are no verdicts here. A site can fit the texts and still be wrong, and the strongest arguments, from place-names and archaeology, are ones I do not try to score. But the tally shows how much the texts constrain, and how far apart the theories are.

The answer to the second question is: very far. The two most distant proposals for Camlann are {{battles.camlann.spread_km|int}} km apart, Badon's {{battles.badon.spread_km|int}} km and Brunanburh's {{battles.brunanburh.spread_km|int}} km. At the other end is a small battle in my own corner of Lancashire. Every theory for the Battle of Whalley in 798 fits inside {{headline.narrowest.km|f1}} km.

The spread is widest where the evidence is most literary. Camlann rests on a single line in the Welsh annals and a name that may mean "crooked bank", so it has been put on the River Camel in Cornwall, on Hadrian's Wall, in Snowdonia, at Salisbury and even in Angus. Badon and Brunanburh are not much better. The tight clusters belong to battles whose sources name a place that still exists: Tettenhall and Wednesfield are {{battles.tettenhall.spread_km|f1}} km apart, and the Whalley theories sit within {{headline.narrowest.km|f1}} km.

03 · The textsWhat do the sources actually say?

Very little. Across the {{n_battles|int}} battles I could find only {{headline.total_checks|int}} clues that can be tested on a map, and two battles, Catraeth and Degsastan, offer none at all: just a name. The commonest clues are a hill, because so many of the names end in dun or come to us in Latin as mons, and a named town nearby. After those come the sea, rivers, steep ground, open ground and a lake.

Badon: a siege on a hill

Gildas, writing within living memory, calls it "the siege of Badon Hill". Bede also calls it a siege. So I asked two things of each site: is it on high ground, and is there a hillfort within reach to besiege? {{battles.badon.n_full_fit|Words}} sites meet both: Liddington Castle above Swindon, Badbury Rings in Dorset, Ringsbury Camp near Braydon and Arbury Hill in Northamptonshire. Bath, Geoffrey of Monmouth's answer, meets neither: the city sits in a valley, and the nearest hillfort in the Atlas is {{extra.bath_fort_km|f1}} km away. That does not rule out the hills above Bath, which score better, but it is a reminder that Geoffrey wrote six centuries after the event and invented freely.

Brunanburh: ships, or the Humber?

The poem in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, written within a generation, says the beaten Norsemen fled by ship over deep water to Dublin. Chroniclers of the twelfth century add that Olaf's fleet sailed into the Humber. These two clues pull in different directions. Sites on the Wirral fit the ships but are {{extra.bromborough_humber_km|int}} km from the Humber; sites inland in the East Riding fit the Humber but are further from open water. Only {{extra.brun_both_full_n|words}}, Barton-upon-Humber and Little Weighton, are close to both. No site meets all four clues; the best score is shared by Bromborough, Londesborough and Nunburnholme. The Humber clue comes from later writers, so I would not give it equal weight, but the table makes the tension plain.

Mons Graupius: a big hill near the sea

Tacitus puts the Caledonians on the slopes of a hill, rising in tiers, while Agricola's fleet works along the coast. Bennachie, the best-known candidate, is the only one with the full {{extra.graupius_relief_pass|int}} m of relief I ask for, but it is {{extra.bennachie_coast_km|int}} km from the sea. The hills near Stonehaven, Kempstone Hill, the Hill of Megray and the camp at Raedykes, are within sight of the sea and score best. Neither clue is strong: Tacitus says the fleet was sent ahead to spread fear, not that it sailed alongside the army.

Nechtansmere: mountains and a lake

Bede says the Northumbrians were lured "into the straits of inaccessible mountains". The Welsh name is Linn Garan, a lake. Dunnichen in Angus, the traditional site, has a drained moss for its lake but only modest hills. Alex Woolf's Dunachton on Loch Insh, in Badenoch, has both a loch and real hills nearby, though not quite the 300 m of relief within 3 km that I set for a full pass. Rescobie Loch, north of Dunnichen Hill, ties with it.

04 · Close to homeWhalley, 798

The battle I know best is the one on my doorstep. Under the year 798 the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle records "a great battle at Whalley in Northumbria" in Lent, on 2 April, in which Alric son of Heardberht was killed "and many others with him". Symeon of Durham, working in the twelfth century from older northern annals, tells us who was fighting: Wada, one of the men who had murdered King Aethelred two years before, led his followers against the new king Eardwulf "in a place called by the English Billingahoh, near Walalege". Wada was beaten and fled.

Whalley is Walalege. Billingahoh is the root of Billington, the next village down the Calder. The Victoria County History goes further: it reads Billingahoh as the name of a hill, the summit that the monks of Whalley later called Belsetenab and that is "now contracted simply to Whalley Nab", the wooded hill that rises across the river from the village. Billington Moor, the long ridge that the Victoria County History says ends at the Nab, is the site named in Wikipedia's article on Eardwulf. And there is local folklore too: the Reverend J. R. Luck, writing in 1896, recorded a story that Wada's dead were buried in the mound at Loe Hill, near the Ribble at Winckley. He dug into it and decided it was natural.

All five sites lie within {{whalley.max_pair_km|f1}} km of each other. The two texts are not rivals: the Chronicle names the nearest well-known place, and Symeon names the exact spot near it. Both clues, near Whalley and on a hill-spur (the Old English hoh), are met in full only at Billington, which sits on the lower slopes below the Nab and the moor. Whalley village meets the hill clue loosely, because Whalley Nab rises within a kilometre of it. The Loe Hill mound, low by the Ribble, fits least well, which matches what its excavator thought of it.

The setting also makes sense of the fight. The Roman road from Ribchester to Ilkley passes about {{whalley.cands.0.road_km|f1}} km from Whalley, and Ribchester itself is {{whalley.to_ribchester_km|f1}} km away. The Calder and the Ribble meet within Billington township. York, where Eardwulf had been crowned in 796, is {{whalley.to_york_km|int}} km to the east. A rebel force on the western edge of Northumbria, near an old road and the river crossings at the foot of a steep hill: that is my reading of the setting, not the texts'. What the data do show is that this is the one lost battle in the set where the argument is about which field, not which county.

05 · The pinsLost, but still on the map

Wikidata, the open database behind many maps and apps, holds {{wikidata.n_battles_bi|int}} battles with coordinates in Britain and Ireland, {{wikidata.n_before_1066|int}} of them dated before 1066. It gives a single exact point for {{wikidata.n_study_pinned|int}} of the {{headline.n_unlocated|int}} lost battles in this study. For Degsastan it records the location as unknown, which is honest. For the rest, {{wikidata.n_pins_within_2km|words}} of the pins sit within 2 km of one proposed site, usually the traditional one: Bath for Badon, Dunnichen for Nechtansmere, Ashingdon for Assandun. Brunanburh's pin is at Burnley, one of the minority theories.

Two pins sit at no proposed site at all. Mons Graupius is pinned in the southern Grampians, {{battles.graupius.wikidata.nearest_km|f1}} km from the nearest place anyone has proposed. And the Battle of Edington, the one disputed battle on which most historians now agree, is pinned {{battles.edington.wikidata.nearest_km|f1}} km from the site given in Wikipedia's own article. A single dot looks like knowledge. For these battles, it is a guess dressed up as a fact.

06 · MethodHow I did it, and what it cannot tell you

The battles. I took {{headline.n_unlocated|int}} battles from Roman and early medieval Britain whose site is unknown or disputed, plus Heavenfield (634) as a control: its site has a continuous tradition, a church and a cross, and both of its clues are met there.

The proposals. I extracted every proposed site named in each battle's English Wikipedia article ({{extra.n_wikipedia_sources|int}} of the {{n_candidates|int}} proposals come from these), and added sites from the Victoria County History of Lancashire, the Scottish Archaeological Research Framework (ScARF), local folklore recorded online and an article on Camlann. Every proposal keeps its source URL. {{n_unplaced|Words}} proposals were too vague to place, such as "Sutherland" or "the Gask Ridge", and are listed but not mapped.

The points. I did not guess any coordinates. {{extra.n_points_os|int}} sites are placed from OS Open Names, {{extra.n_points_atlas|int}} from the Atlas of Hillforts, {{extra.n_points_grid|int}} from grid references given in a source, {{extra.n_points_coord|int}} from coordinates given in a source, {{extra.n_points_rivers|int}} on rivers from OS Open Rivers and {{extra.n_points_pleiades|int}} from Pleiades. Where a proposal names a village, the dot is the village; where it names a river, the dot is the middle of the river.

The measures. For each point: ground height and whether it, or the highest ground within 1 km, is in the top 10% (or 25%) of the ground within 2 to 3 km; relief (highest minus lowest ground) within 1 km and 3 km; distance to the sea, to the nearest named river, to the nearest named lake, to the nearest Roman road in Itiner-e (not available north of the Forth), and to the nearest hillfort in the Atlas of Hillforts.

The checks. Each clue becomes one check with a stated threshold, shown in the battle card above. A check scores 1 if met, ½ if met loosely, 0 if not; a site's fit is the average. The thresholds are mine and are deliberately simple. Changing them would change some ranks.

Caveats

This is a tally, not a verdict. It ignores the evidence that drives most identifications: place-name philology, archaeology, charters and local tradition. It gives a twelfth-century chronicle the same weight as a contemporary poem. The landscape has changed: lakes have been drained, woods cleared and coasts moved, and the elevation model smooths summits. The list of proposals depends on what Wikipedia's editors chose to include. And Wikidata's coverage of battles differs a great deal from country to country, so the {{wikidata.n_battles_bi|int}} battles it places in Britain and Ireland are not a complete count.
